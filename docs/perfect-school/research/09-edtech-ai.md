# EdTech and AI in Disadvantaged Schools: What the Evidence Supports (and the Hype It Does Not)

*Research brief 09 — school design programme. Drafted July 2026. UK policy position verified against 2025–26 sources.*

## What the evidence says

**Technology is a delivery mechanism, not an intervention.** The EEF's foundational guidance ([Using Digital Technology to Improve Learning](https://educationendowmentfoundation.org.uk/education-evidence/guidance-reports/digital)) is blunt: start with the pedagogical problem, not the product, because technology only improves outcomes when it amplifies an already-effective teaching practice (explanation, practice, feedback, assessment). The EEF's 2025 systematic review and meta-analysis, [Understanding Quality Characteristics of EdTech Interventions and Implementation for Disadvantaged Pupils](https://educationendowmentfoundation.org.uk/education-evidence/evidence-reviews/edtech-interventions-for-disadvantaged-pupils) ([full report](https://eric.ed.gov/?id=ED677254)), found an average positive effect of EdTech interventions on attainment (pooled d ≈ 0.40, larger in secondary, d ≈ 0.57, than primary, d ≈ 0.34) — but the average effect for socio-economically disadvantaged pupils was **lower** than the overall average. The EEF's own framing: EdTech is a ["double-edged sword"](https://educationendowmentfoundation.org.uk/news/effectiveness-of-edtech-reflections-from-new-review) that can narrow or widen the gap depending on tool selection and implementation. Mechanisms associated with impact include scaffolded feedback, spaced/adaptive practice and structured collaborative learning — i.e., the same active ingredients as good teaching.

**AI: real workload evidence, weak attainment evidence.** The strongest 2024–26 UK evidence is about **teacher time**, not pupil outcomes. Oak National Academy's Aila lesson assistant shows [early evaluation evidence](https://www.thenational.academy/blog/how-is-aila-impacting-teacher-lesson-planning-practices-workload-and-expertise-early-insights) of ~3 hours/week average reported savings, with ~two-thirds of teachers reporting reduced workload and plan quality rated high; a proper test — an [EEF/NFER randomised Teacher Choices trial](https://educationendowmentfoundation.org.uk/projects-and-evaluation/projects/aila-teacher-choices-trial) with ~450 KS2 teachers in 86 schools — reports **autumn 2026**. On pupil-facing AI tutoring, [Ofsted's June 2025 early-adopters study](https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/ai-in-schools-and-further-education-findings-from-early-adopters/the-biggest-risk-is-doing-nothing-insights-from-early-adopters-of-artificial-intelligence-in-schools-and-further-education-colleges) concluded that robust evidence of AI's impact on outcomes is "explorative, short-term and in limited domains", and [the government itself concedes evidence for AI tutoring is "limited"](https://www.tes.com/magazine/news/general/limited-evidence-ai-tutoring-tools-government-admits) even as it funds it. An exploratory [RCT of LearnLM-based tutoring in UK classrooms](https://arxiv.org/html/2512.23633v1) (2025) suggests AI tutoring can be safe and modestly effective, but it is one small trial. Treat "AI tutor = Bloom's two-sigma" claims as hype.

**What has evidence at scale is human-designed, adaptive practice.** The independent Cambridge/RAND analysis of [Sparx Maths](https://www.educ.cam.ac.uk/research/programmes/sparx/) found ~1 hour/week of completed Sparx homework over a year associated with roughly **+20–30% of a GCSE grade** — but crucially, *access alone had no effect*; only sustained completion did (correlational, so flag accordingly). The [EEF Toolkit](https://educationendowmentfoundation.org.uk/education-evidence/teaching-learning-toolkit/small-group-tuition) rates homework at secondary at **+5 months** (low evidence security) and one-to-one tuition at **+4–6 months**. The [Maths-Whizz EEF trial](https://educationendowmentfoundation.org.uk/projects-and-evaluation/promising-programmes/maths-whizz-intelligent-tutoring) found +1 month overall and **+2 months for FSM pupils** — one of the few EdTech trials where disadvantaged pupils gained more. Eedi's [2023–24 RCT](https://www.eedi.com/news/new-uk-study-finds-students-using-eedi-gain-2-4-months-of-additional-maths-progress) (2,901 Y7s, 20 schools; commissioned by Eedi, so not fully independent) reported +2 months on average, +4 months for pupils doing 10–15 min/week.

**The digital divide is now an AI divide.** Ofcom estimated [1.8m children lacked adequate device access](https://www.suttontrust.com/our-research/remote-learning-the-digital-divide/) at the start of the pandemic. The Sutton Trust's July 2025 report [*Artificial Advantage?*](https://www.suttontrust.com/news-opinion/all-news-opinion/state-schools-falling-behind-in-new-ai-digital-divide/) (Teacher Tapp, n>10,000) found private-school teachers are more than twice as likely to have formal AI training (45% vs 21%), three times as likely to work in a school with an AI strategy (27% vs 9%) — and *within* the state sector, the most deprived schools trail the most affluent (formal training 18% vs 26%). The [DfE Technology in Schools Survey 2024–25](https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/technology-in-schools-survey-report-2024-to-2025) shows infrastructure improving but leader-reported staff confidence in technology **falling** (secondary: 58%→44% since 2023), with 85% of teachers citing budget as a barrier and home device/connectivity gaps persisting.

## What the best operators actually do

- **Centralised curriculum spine, locally adapted.** [United Learning](https://unitedlearning.org.uk/) (90+ schools) provides fully-resourced central lessons, knowledge organisers and glossaries via its [United Curriculum platform](https://unitedcurriculum.org.uk/), explicitly so teachers spend time adapting rather than authoring, extending "access to the curriculum beyond the school day". [Star Academies](https://staracademies.org/working-at-star/learning-and-development/) (top Progress 8 outcomes in highly deprived catchments) runs a central digital Learning Hub for staff development. The pattern: centralise the resource base, keep adaptation professional and local.
- **Oak as free national infrastructure — with caveats.** Oak is now [used in roughly three-quarters of England's schools (~200,000 teachers)](https://www.thenational.academy/blog/five-key-insights-from-our-2425-impact-report), with a median reported saving of ~4 hrs/week among teachers reporting reduced workload. The [September 2025 independent review](https://assets.publishing.service.gov.uk/media/68d54085c908572e81248ce8/Oak_National_Academy_Independent_Review.pdf) confirmed Oak should remain **optional and adaptable**, found only "modest" market distortion, and recommended modest further investment; the [government's response to the Curriculum & Assessment Review](https://assets.publishing.service.gov.uk/media/690b2a4a14b040dfe82922ea/Government_response_to_the_Curriculum_and_Assessment_Review.pdf) (Nov 2025) confirms Oak will keep developing curriculum materials and AI tools aligned to the reformed curriculum.
- **Workload-first AI adoption with governance.** [Ofsted's early adopters](https://www.gov.uk/government/news/ai-in-education-how-schools-and-further-education-colleges-are-making-it-work) (21 schools/MATs/colleges, spring 2025) adopted AI primarily to cut workload (planning, resources, parent communications, adapting materials for EAL/SEND pupils), appointed a named **AI champion**, ran cross-functional AI committees, and addressed data protection, bias and safeguarding up front.
- **Riding the national programmes rather than buying blind.** The DfE has built a [£3m curriculum "content store"](https://schoolsweek.co.uk/3m-government-ai-content-store-to-help-teachers-plan-lessons/) for AI developers, an [EdTech Evidence Board with the Chartered College and an EdTech Impact Testbed](https://roadmap-for-modern-digital-government.campaign.gov.uk/ai/ai-in-education/), and an [AI Tutoring Pioneer programme](https://www.gov.uk/government/news/450000-disadvantaged-pupils-could-benefit-from-ai-tutoring-tools) — up to 8 pioneers at £300k each, targeting up to **450,000 disadvantaged Year 9–11 pupils** in English, maths, science and MFL, with classroom trials from autumn 2026 and tools available by end of 2027. Smart operators join these trials (free tools + evaluation support) rather than purchasing unproven products.

## Design implications for our school

1. **Pedagogy-first procurement rule.** No EdTech purchase without a named teaching problem, an evidence check (EEF review/Evidence Board), and a usage-based evaluation plan. Budget assumes most products deliver nothing without implementation effort.
2. **Adopt a centralised digital curriculum spine from day one** (Oak and/or United Curriculum as the base), with teacher time protected for *adaptation to our SEND profile*, not authoring from scratch. This is the highest-confidence workload win available (≈3–4 hrs/teacher/week self-reported).
3. **Equity audit at intake:** device + broadband survey for every family; school-owned loan laptops and 4G/5G data SIMs for the gap; supervised after-school "homework club" so platform homework never depends on home access. Sparx-style gains accrue only to pupils who *complete* the work — the school must engineer completion, not just licences.
4. **One adaptive practice platform per core subject, ruthlessly monitored on usage** (minutes/week per pupil, split by PP/SEND), because access without engagement has zero effect.
5. **AI for staff workload now; pupil-facing AI tutoring only inside evaluated trials.** Join the DfE AI Tutoring Pioneer trials (autumn 2026) and EEF Aila trial pipeline rather than buying commercial "AI tutors" ahead of evidence.
6. **Appoint an AI champion + cross-functional AI governance group** (Ofsted early-adopter model), with a published AI policy covering data protection, safeguarding and academic integrity — putting our deprived-catchment school in the 9%, not the 91%.
7. **Invest in staff CPD, not just kit:** the binding constraint in 2025–26 data is falling staff confidence, and the AI training gap is a class gap.
8. **Cheap wins first:** parental text-messaging about tests/homework (+1 month, ≤£6/pupil) before any five-figure platform.
9. **Assistive/adaptive uses for SEND** (text-to-speech, dictation, AI-adapted resources for EAL/SEND) are among the most concrete benefits early adopters report — build them into universal (Wave 1) provision.
10. **Plan for the reformed curriculum:** digital literacy strengthened at every key stage and a broader Computing GCSE (incl. AI) per the [Curriculum & Assessment Review](https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/curriculum-and-assessment-review-final-report) — design the timetable and staffing for it now.
