# Department-Level Curriculum, Assessment and Feedback Operations (2025–26)

Research brief one level below the whole-school blueprint (see `0-canon.md`): concrete, classroom- and department-sized practice — schemes of work, daily retrieval, low-stakes quizzing, mastery checkpoints and, critically, workload-reducing feedback/marking policy. Worked example throughout: a KS3 science department (Adam's TLR3 remit), generalisable to any subject.

## What the evidence says

**Retrieval practice is one of the two "high utility" learning strategies identified by cognitive science, and it is cheap.** Dunlosky et al.'s (2013) review of ten study techniques rated practice testing and distributed practice as the only two "high utility" strategies for classroom use; a 2021 systematic review of 49 classroom effect sizes (n=5,374) found 57% showed medium-to-large benefits, with classroom effects (~0.5) close to lab effects (0.62) ([Agarwal et al. 2021, *Educational Psychology Review*](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10648-021-09595-9); [Dunlosky 2013 summary](https://www.structural-learning.com/post/testing-effect-retrieval-practice)). Rosenshine's *Principles of Instruction* independently converges on the same point: daily review, weekly and monthly review, and frequent low-stakes questioning are core to effective teaching ([EEF summary of Rosenshine](https://educationendowmentfoundation.org.uk/education-evidence/guidance-reports)).

**Formative, low-stakes assessment moves attainment; high-stakes marking of every book does not.** The EEF's *Embedding Formative Assessment* RCT (140 schools, 25,000 pupils) found +2 months on Attainment 8 at very low cost, concentrated among the lowest-prior-attainment third ([EEF](https://educationendowmentfoundation.org.uk/projects-and-evaluation/projects/embedding-formative-assessment)). The EEF's dedicated *Teacher Feedback to Improve Pupil Learning* guidance report (Nov 2021) is explicit that **written marking is only one form of feedback, and often not the best one**: Recommendation 4 states schools should use "purposeful and time-efficient" written feedback, naming **live marking** (feedback given and recorded during the lesson) and **coded/whole-class marking** as evidence-consistent alternatives to exhaustive written comments, because extensive marking has a large opportunity cost against planning and subject-knowledge development ([EEF Feedback guidance, Recommendation 4](https://educationendowmentfoundation.org.uk/education-evidence/guidance-reports/feedback); [Durrington Research School summary](https://researchschool.org.uk/durrington/news/summary-of-the-eefs-new-guidance-report-teacher-feedback-to-improve-pupil-learning)). The EEF Toolkit rates feedback as high-impact (~+6 months) at very low cost ([EEF Toolkit: Feedback](https://educationendowmentfoundation.org.uk/education-evidence/teaching-learning-toolkit/feedback)).

**This is government policy, not just researcher preference.** The DfE's 2016 Marking Policy Review Group — convened after the Workload Challenge survey identified marking as one of the three most burdensome tasks in teaching — concluded that "detailed written marking of every piece of work" is not the most effective form of feedback, and that marking "has evolved into an unhelpful burden... when the time it takes is not repaid in positive impact on pupils' progress" ([DfE Reducing Teacher Workload](https://assets.publishing.service.gov.uk/government/uploads/system/uploads/attachment_data/file/594027/6.2798_DFE_MB_Reducing_Teacher_Workload_Pamphlet_20161207_print.pdf)). Ofsted's own position, restated consistently since, is that **inspectors do not expect any specific frequency, type or volume of marking, nor any written record of verbal feedback** — schools set their own policy ([Ofsted school inspection handbook, marking clarification](https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/school-inspection-handbook-eif); corroborated via [CIEA summary](https://www.herts.ac.uk/ciea/my-ciea/articles/reducing-teacher-workload-around-marking)). This closes off the common excuse ("Ofsted wants to see marked books") that drives workload-heavy policies.

**Homework works best as retrieval practice on already-taught content, not new independent learning.** The EEF Toolkit finds homework worth ~+5 months at secondary (vs +3 months at primary), with effects strongest when homework is "an integral part of learning" — explicitly linked to what happens in lesson — rather than a bolt-on extension task; digital-technology-supported homework (e.g., quizzing platforms) shows some of the largest effects (~+6 months) ([EEF Toolkit: Homework](https://educationendowmentfoundation.org.uk/education-evidence/teaching-learning-toolkit/homework)).

**Evidence caveats, stated honestly.** The bulk of retrieval-practice effect sizes still come from lab studies (223) rather than classrooms (30) — the classroom evidence base, while consistent, is thinner. The EFA trial's headline effect was modest (ES≈0.10) and only significant at the 10% level in the primary model. EEF homework effect sizes are correlational meta-analytic averages across heterogeneous study designs, not a single RCT, and "homework" varies hugely in what was actually set. No causal RCT directly compares whole-class feedback against traditional marking on attainment — the case for whole-class feedback rests on workload-neutral-or-better outcomes plus the general feedback evidence, not a dedicated trial.

## What the best operators actually do (named, concrete)

- **Michaela Community School**: every subject runs a **weekly quiz** on the current knowledge organiser plus cumulative recall of prior organisers/vocabulary, set as homework and quizzed in the following lesson — explicitly designed to "impede the forgetting curve" through spaced, cumulative retrieval; supplemented by **biannual exams in January and June** ([Schools Week: Homework habits at Michaela](https://schoolsweek.co.uk/homework-habits-what-we-have-learned-at-michaela/)). Knowledge organisers themselves were developed at Michaela by Joe Kirby (2015) as single-page, dense summaries of facts/vocabulary/dates per topic, built to be the object pupils self-quiz from at home and teachers quiz from in class ([Joe Kirby, "Knowledge Organisers"](https://joe-kirby.com/2015/03/28/knowledge-organisers/); [Alice Leung on KOs and retrieval](https://aliceleung.net/2020/01/25/using-knowledge-organisers-to-support-retrieval-practice/)).
- **Dixons Academies Trust**: daily formative assessment through targeted questioning, low-stakes quizzing and "Show Call" (cold-calling a pupil's work onto the visualiser), combined with **"messy marking"** — whole-class feedback delivered live or via a single annotated exemplar, with pupils responding in green pen — rather than comment-by-comment marking of every book ([Dixons Trinity Chapeltown: assessment](https://www.dixonstc.com/about/assessment); [Dixons: powerful feedback](https://www.dixonstc.com/why/powerful-feedback)).
- **Whole-class feedback (WCF) sheets**, popularised by Craig Barton/Jo Facer and now near-universal in evidence-informed departments: teacher reads a set of books/scripts without writing in them, records common errors/misconceptions/exemplary answers on **one single sheet**, then the whole class works through corrections together in the next lesson — cutting marking time from ~1 minute/book to a few minutes for 30 books, while pupils get feedback the next lesson rather than a week later ([Tips for Teachers: Whole-Class Feedback](https://tipsforteachers.co.uk/whole-class-feedback/); [Chartered College: WCF as our weapon against workload](https://my.chartered.college/research-hub/q-how-can-we-reduce-teacher-workload-without-affecting-the-quality-of-marking-a-whole-class-feedback/)).
- **United Learning**: trust-wide standardised assessment twice yearly in Y7–8 across six subjects including science, nationally benchmarked with question-level analysis via Smartgrade — the mastery-checkpoint layer sits above weekly in-department quizzing, not instead of it ([United Curriculum: assessment](https://unitedcurriculum.org.uk/assessment)).
- **Ark and Star Academies science departments** (consistent with published curriculum documents) sequence KS3 science into half-termly units, each with its own knowledge organiser, a end-of-unit assessment, and a cumulative "Do Now" retrieval starter in every lesson drawing on the current and two prior units — the standard "low-stakes quiz every lesson, formal checkpoint every half-term" cadence found across high-performing MATs.

## Design implications for our department layer

1. **Every lesson opens with a 5-minute "Do Now" retrieval starter** (4–6 questions: 2 from the current unit, 2 from the prior unit, 1–2 interleaved from a unit 6+ months back) — cheap, evidenced (Dunlosky/Agarwal), and the single highest-leverage routine a HoD can mandate trust-wide without extra marking. Self-marked against a visualiser answer in under 2 minutes; teacher circulates and notes patterns, not scores.
2. **One knowledge organiser per half-termly unit**, one side of A4, covering core facts/vocabulary/diagrams — the single artefact that drives homework self-quizzing, lesson-starter content and end-of-unit assessment content, so pupils, TAs and cover teachers all work from the same source (Michaela/Kirby model).
3. **Weekly low-stakes quiz** (10 minutes, self- or peer-marked in lesson, recorded as a class-average dot on a tracker, never as an individual written comment) on the current + cumulative knowledge organisers. Zero marking-at-home burden.
4. **Homework = self-quizzing on the knowledge organiser**, not new independent tasks — a Frayer-style app/paper booklet the pupil completes and self-marks, checked (not marked) by the teacher for completion. This captures the EEF's "integral to learning, not a bolt-on" homework effect (+5 months secondary) at near-zero marking cost.
5. **Whole-class feedback (WCF), not individual written marking, as the department default** for extended writing/6-mark answers: teacher reads a stack once, populates a single WCF sheet (common misconceptions, 2 exemplar answers, a "next step" instruction), pupils redraft against it next lesson in a different colour pen. Live/verbal marking during independent work is the default for day-to-day exercise-book work; written comment-by-comment marking is reserved for a termly deep-dive sample (e.g., 6 books/class/term) for QA and moderation, not universal practice — matching both the EEF feedback guidance and the DfE Marking Policy Review, and defensible against Ofsted because inspectors do not require any specific marking volume or frequency.
6. **Half-termly mastery checkpoint** (20–30 minutes, closed-book, covering the unit just finished plus a cumulative 20% from prior units) feeds a simple RAG tracker per pupil per objective; any pupil below the mastery threshold is flagged into the department's responsive re-teach slot (built into the following week's Do Now rotation and, for persistent gaps, the whole-school 15:30–16:30 directed block per Canon §2) rather than triggering re-marking of the original assessment.
7. **Twice-yearly standardised, trust/department-benchmarked assessment** (end of Y7–9 terms, mirroring United Learning's model) gives comparable, question-level data across classes and — eventually — across schools, sitting above the weekly/half-termly rhythm as the mastery-graph input, not replacing it.
8. **Explicit workload ceiling in the department handbook**: no subject requires written comments on every piece of work; WCF sheets and live marking are the stated, defensible default, citing the DfE 2016 review and Ofsted's no-required-marking-volume position directly in the policy document, so new/anxious staff and parents both have a citable answer.
