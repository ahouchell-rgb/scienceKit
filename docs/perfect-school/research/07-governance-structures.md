# Governance and Structural Options for a New School in England, 2025–26

**Brief 07 — Governance, structures, how new schools open now, and funding.** Researched July 2026. The landscape has moved fast since 2024: the Children's Wellbeing and Schools Act received Royal Assent in May 2026, the free-schools presumption has been scrapped, Ofsted report cards went live in November 2025, and a Schools White Paper (February 2026) is reshaping SEND. This brief reflects the post-2024 settlement, not pre-2024 assumptions.

## What the evidence says

**Structure per se does not drive outcomes; the quality of the group does.** The Education Policy Institute's long-running analyses of school groups find *no systematic difference* between multi-academy trusts (MATs) and local authorities (LAs) as categories — variation *within* each type dwarfs variation between types. At secondary, the gap between the top- and bottom-performing school groups is worth roughly 5 GCSE grades across a pupil's subjects ([EPI 2018](https://epi.org.uk/report/school-performance-multi-academy-trusts-local-authorities/); [EPI, *The Features of Effective School Groups*, 2024](https://epi.org.uk/publications-and-research/the-features-of-effective-school-groups/)).

**For disadvantaged pupils the picture is double-edged.** EPI's 2024 study found larger MATs (10+ schools) enrol more disadvantaged pupils and achieve better attainment outcomes for them — but also show higher persistent absence, suspension rates and unexplained pupil exits, and higher secondary teacher turnover (16.9% median MAT vs 14.4% median LA; ~19.5% in large MATs). EPI's explicit recommendation: judge school groups on **inclusion as well as attainment** ([EPI 2024 report PDF](https://epi.org.uk/wp-content/uploads/2024/04/Effective_school_groups_final-240404-2.pdf); [Tes coverage](https://www.tes.com/magazine/news/general/judge-school-groups-inclusion-says-epi)). For a high-SEND, high-deprivation catchment this is the central governance risk: the structures that maximise headline attainment can also be the ones that quietly move vulnerable pupils out.

**The legal landscape converged in 2026.** The Children's Wellbeing and Schools Act 2026 (Royal Assent May 2026) substantially narrows the regulatory differences between academies and maintained schools ([Schools Week summary](https://schoolsweek.co.uk/childrens-wellbeing-and-schools-act-the-policies-signed-into-law/); [Bill page](https://bills.parliament.uk/bills/3909)):

- **Curriculum:** academies must follow the national curriculum once the post-Curriculum-and-Assessment-Review curriculum is in place.
- **Teachers:** from 1 September 2027, new academy teachers must hold or be working towards QTS with statutory induction; the Secretary of State can set a pay floor for academies.
- **Admissions:** from June 2026 academies must cooperate with councils on admissions and place planning; councils can direct academies to admit pupils — a significant protection for hard-to-place SEND and vulnerable children.
- **Accountability:** the Secretary of State gains direction powers over non-compliant trusts (June 2026); Ofsted gains a duty to inspect MATs directly (2027 at earliest), with powers to enter any trust premises.
- **New schools:** from 1 September 2026 the "free school presumption" is abolished — councils may again publish proposals for their own community (maintained) schools; automatic academy orders for failing maintained schools are also removed.

**How new schools open now.** The centrally-run wave-based free school programme is effectively over: the October 2024 review of 44 unopened mainstream free schools ended in December 2025 with ~28 minded-to-cancel and only 16 proceeding; only 15 of 92 planned special/AP free schools are definitely going ahead, with councils offered per-pupil cash instead, saving ~£600m and accompanied by £3bn over four years for councils to create specialist places ([Schools Week](https://schoolsweek.co.uk/nightmare-before-christmas-46-free-school-projects-scrapped-and-58-special-schools-in-limbo/); [Hansard, 22 Oct 2024](https://hansard.parliament.uk/commons/2024-10-22/debates/24102240000014/MainstreamFreeSchoolsReview)). Until September 2026 a basic-need new school goes through the LA-run **free school presumption** competition (LA finds site and capital, specifies the school, runs an open trust competition, DfE Regional Director approves the trust) ([DfE guidance](https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/establishing-a-new-school-free-school-presumption)). After September 2026 the LA can alternatively propose a maintained school itself. Practically, a new school in 2026–27 therefore has **two live routes: an LA presumption-style competition won by an academy trust, or an LA-published proposal for a maintained school** — both requiring demonstrated basic need and LA capital.

**Evidence caveats, stated honestly.** The free-school and academisation effect literature is weak and contested: NFER's fifteen-year review finds no clear systematic outcome advantage for free schools once intake is controlled ([NFER blog](https://www.nfer.ac.uk/blogs/fifteen-years-of-free-schools-and-better-outcomes-for-pupils/)). EPI's group comparisons are non-experimental; selection into trusts is not random. No governance structure has an EEF-style effect size — governance operates through curriculum, teaching and inclusion, which do.

## What the best operators actually do

- **High-performing trusts centralise the right things.** The strongest groups in EPI's analysis combine scale (10+ schools, regional clustering) with shared curriculum, centralised school-improvement capacity and central services — while the weakest groups show that scale alone guarantees nothing.
- **They comply with, and exceed, the governance baseline.** The [Academy Trust Handbook 2025](https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/academy-trust-handbook/academy-trust-handbook-2025-effective-from-1-september-2025) (effective 1 Sept 2025) requires a CFO, an audit and risk committee, documented and "defensible" executive pay policy, internal scrutiny, no ransom payments after cyber attack, and progress towards the six DfE digital standards by 2030. The [DfE academy trust governance guide](https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/academy-trust-governance-guide/academy-trust-governance-guide) sets the best-practice layer: clear separation of members (≥5, majority independent of the board), trustees, and executive; scheme of delegation; local governing committees that keep parental and community voice.
- **They plan for MAT-level Ofsted inspection.** From November 2025 schools receive **report cards**: a 5-point colour-coded scale ("exceptional" → "urgent improvement") across six areas — *inclusion*, curriculum and teaching, achievement, attendance and behaviour, personal development and wellbeing, leadership and governance — plus a separate safeguarding met/not-met ([Ofsted/GOV.UK](https://www.gov.uk/government/news/ofsted-confirms-changes-to-education-inspection-and-unveils-new-look-report-cards)). Inclusion is now a *graded* judgement — structurally rewarding exactly what a high-SEND school should be built to do. CWS Act MAT inspections (from ~2027) mean trust boards will be inspected on the same logic.
- **They position for the February 2026 Schools White Paper SEND reforms:** a decade-long shift to mainstream inclusion, tiered support (universal / targeted / targeted-plus / specialist), digital Individual Support Plans for all identified SEND, EHCPs reserved for the most complex needs by ~2035 (no EHCP changes before September 2030), ~£4bn for mainstream inclusion over three years including ~£1.8bn for the "Experts at Hand" local specialist service ([Commons Library CBP-10550](https://commonslibrary.parliament.uk/research-briefings/cbp-10550/); [DfE Education Hub](https://educationhub.blog.gov.uk/2026/05/schools-white-paper-what-parents-need-to-know-about-changes-to-the-send-system/)).

## Numbers that matter (effect sizes, costs, ratios, outcomes)

**2026–27 schools NFF factor values** ([DfE schools block funding formulae 2026-27](https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/schools-block-funding-formulae-2026-to-2027/schools-block-funding-formulae-2026-to-2027); [NFF summary policy document](https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/pre-16-schools-funding-local-authority-guidance-for-2026-to-2027/schools-national-funding-formula-nff-summary-policy-document-for-2026-to-2027)):

| Factor | 2026–27 value |
|---|---|
| Basic entitlement: primary / KS3 / KS4 | £4,064 / £5,686 / £6,410 |
| FSM (current) | £505 |
| FSM6: primary / secondary | £1,210 / £1,725 |
| IDACI secondary, bands A→F | £970 / £760 / £710 / £650 / £460 / £345 |
| Low prior attainment: primary / secondary | £1,200 / £1,825 |
| EAL: primary / secondary | £610 / £1,630 |
| Lump sum (per school) | £152,700 |
| Minimum per-pupil level: primary / KS3 / KS4 / secondary | £5,115 / £6,388 / £7,018 / £6,640 |

National average NFF funding rises to **£6,771 per pupil** in 2026–27 (from £6,608); ~11% of all NFF funding flows through deprivation factors; 91.9% is pupil-led.

**Pupil premium 2025–26:** £1,075 per FSM6 secondary pupil (£1,515 primary); £2,630 for looked-after/previously looked-after ([GOV.UK](https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/pupil-premium/pupil-premium); [Commons Library](https://commonslibrary.parliament.uk/research-briefings/sn06700/)).

**SEND funding:** mainstream schools fund the first **£6,000** of additional support per pupil from the **notional SEN budget** (identified within the delegated budget/GAG; LAs must sanity-check it annually); costs above £6,000 attract LA high-needs **top-up funding**, EHCP or not ([notional SEN budget operational guidance 2026-27](https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/pre-16-schools-funding-local-authority-guidance-for-2026-to-2027/the-notional-sen-budget-for-mainstream-schools-operational-guidance-2026-to-2027)). The national high-needs budget is ~**£12bn** and the high-needs NFF is **suspended for 2026–27** (allocations rolled forward from 2025–26 with adjustments) pending SEND reform ([high needs operational guide 2026-27](https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/high-needs-funding-arrangements-2026-to-2027/high-needs-funding-2026-to-2027-operational-guide)) — a real planning risk for a high-SEND school.

**Illustrative budget for our school** (1,200-place 11–16 secondary; ~45% FSM6, ~30% current FSM, most pupils IDACI bands A–C, ~40% low prior attainment): basic entitlement ≈ £7.2m; deprivation factors ≈ £1.3–1.5m; low prior attainment ≈ £0.9m; EAL, mobility, lump sum ≈ £0.4m → core NFF income plausibly **£8,200–£8,800 per pupil (~£10–10.5m)**, well above the £6,771 average, plus **~£580k pupil premium**, plus notional SEN budget within that total and per-pupil high-needs top-ups (typically £4,000–£10,000+ above the £6k threshold per EHCP pupil). Treat as illustrative: final allocations flow through the LA's local formula, and LAs still set local formulae within NFF constraints.

## Design implications for our school

1. **Choose the operator, not the badge.** Post-CWS Act, curriculum, QTS, pay-floor and admissions rules apply to academies too; the MAT-vs-maintained decision is now mostly about school-improvement capacity, shared services and governance quality — where the evidence says variation within each type is what matters.
2. **Two realistic routes to open:** win an LA presumption-style competition as (or with) a strong inclusion-focused trust, or — from September 2026 — partner with the LA on a council-proposed maintained school. Budget on the LA providing site and capital either way; the central free-school wave route is gone.
3. **If MAT: join or form a mid-to-large, regionally clustered trust with an explicit inclusion covenant** — published commitments on zero unexplained exits, suspension-rate transparency and admitting directed pupils — to capture the large-MAT attainment advantage for disadvantaged pupils while contracting away its documented exclusion risk.
4. **Design governance for the 2025 Handbook and MAT-level Ofsted from day one:** independent members, skills-based trustee board, audit & risk committee, CFO, defensible pay policy, local governing body with parent/community/SEND-parent seats, and board KPIs mirroring the six report-card areas — with *inclusion* first.
5. **Build the financial model on deprivation-weighted NFF income (~£8.2–8.8k/pupil core + ~£1.1k PP for ~45% of pupils),** but stress-test against a frozen high-needs block: assume top-up rates flat in cash terms to 2028 and design SEND provision (e.g., a SEN unit / resourced provision, "targeted-plus" tier) that is viable largely within the notional SEN budget.
6. **Align the SEND offer with the White Paper direction now:** tiered universal→specialist support, digital individual support plans, and early engagement with the LA's "Experts at Hand" commissioning — this both serves the catchment and positions the school for the £4bn mainstream-inclusion funding stream.
